Founder journey · Physical consumer product

From market gap to product launch.

A four-year journey of researching, developing and commercialising an original physical product through Amazon.

Amazon FBA stands for Fulfilment by Amazon. Sellers develop and market their own products, while Amazon handles storage, shipping and customer service.

The best product was not one I could source. It was one I had to build myself.

01

Finding the opportunity

The numbers showed demand. Customer reviews showed what was missing.

I used Helium 10 to understand search volume, estimated market size, competitor revenue and demand around different product categories.

The data was useful, but it was only a starting point. Automated opportunity tools show the same markets to every seller. I also browsed Amazon manually, explored unfamiliar product categories and read competitor reviews to understand where existing products disappointed customers.

The product gap

Existing love bridges solved one problem and created another.

Available products often sat on top of the mattresses. They could create a raised area in the middle, reduce comfort or wear out quickly.

I wanted the mattresses to remain the main sleeping surface. The connection needed to work without adding a thick layer on top.

02

Building the product

A sketch became a prototype. The prototype became a manufactured product.

No suitable off-the-shelf product matched the concept, so I started with a drawing and sourced the materials myself.

I found a local tailor who was willing to help turn the idea into a working prototype. Several rounds followed before the construction and material combination felt right.

A limitation

Finding test users was harder than expected.

I tried recruiting through Facebook groups and LinkedIn, but the target group was small and the problem was not something people commonly discussed publicly.

I eventually moved forward with the prototype, my market research and the feedback already visible in competitor reviews. The first broader validation came from customers after launch.

03

Product economics

The retail price came first. Supplier costs had to work backwards from it.

The product was more premium than existing alternatives, but the final price still needed to feel reasonable to customers. I defined the target selling price, margin and import costs before selecting a supplier.

Building the brand

Seidenfeld needed to feel as considered as the product.

I created the brand direction, logo, colour palette and visual identity, then worked with external specialists on packaging and listing design.

The identity was built around simplicity, reliability and understated elegance.

04

Bringing it to market

Production, brand and launch preparation ran in parallel.

While the first production batch was being manufactured and imported, I prepared the Amazon listing and launch.

01

Product photography

The flexible product could not be represented convincingly with a standard 3D render, so I organised a full-day photoshoot at home.

02

Growth and disruption

The launch worked. Then the sales account was suspended.

The first months brought strong sales, positive reviews and an earlier-than-expected stockout.

Amazon later restricted the account after a revenue threshold triggered an additional company verification. The reason was not clearly communicated, and resolving the case took several months.

October 2024 Product launch
First months Positive reviews and rapid stockout
Interruption Account verification and sales suspension
Recovery Sales restored after company verification
2026 Business discontinued

During the interruption, rankings and review performance weakened. Once selling resumed, advertising costs were substantially higher and the business no longer reached the previous level of profitability.

Reflection

A good product still depends on the system around it.

Amazon gave the product access to customers, fulfilment infrastructure and traffic that would have been difficult to build independently.

It also meant that one platform controlled access to the market. The suspension showed how quickly a commercially successful product can be affected by decisions outside the seller's control.

Building Seidenfeld gave me direct experience across discovery, product development, pricing, manufacturing, branding, marketing and commercial decision-making. It also changed how I think about platform risk and sustainable growth.
